# Aircraft

Aircraft are fictional and designed around gameplay roles.

They may be inspired by real-world aircraft categories, but names, stats, balance, manufacturers, and progression are custom. This avoids licensing issues and allows aircraft to fit the game’s story and progression.

Aircraft should teach the player strategy over time. Bigger aircraft are not automatically better. The right aircraft depends on route demand, range, airport limitations, acquisition cost, operating cost, reliability, passenger expectations, schedule needs, and business stage.

## Design Goals

Aircraft should:

- Support clear progression from tiny founder operations to larger airline operations
- Teach different operational roles
- Make fleet decisions meaningful
- Create tradeoffs between cost, capacity, range, speed, reliability, comfort, and risk
- Support leasing, buying, financing, used aircraft, and partner-provided aircraft
- Allow future expansion into interiors, fleet commonality, maintenance depth, and airline strategy

## Aircraft Progression Categories

### Founder Aircraft

Tiny aircraft used in the earliest stage of the game.

These aircraft represent the founder personally getting the operation started. They are cheap compared to later aircraft, but limited in passenger capacity, range, comfort, and airline growth potential.

Typical role:

- First passenger services
- Very short routes
- Tiny airports
- Early contracts
- Learning basic operations

Approximate capacity:

- 6 to 12 passengers

### Commuter Aircraft

Small aircraft used for early scheduled passenger service.

These aircraft help the player move from founder-operated flying into something that resembles a tiny airline.

Typical role:

- Thin scheduled routes
- Community airport service
- Early commuter operations
- Low-cost route experiments

Approximate capacity:

- 9 to 19 passengers

### Small Regional Turboprop

Small turboprops for short regional routes.

They are more airline-like than founder aircraft but still useful for thin markets and smaller airports.

Typical role:

- Short routes
- Small communities
- Early scheduled network growth
- Lower-speed but efficient flying

Approximate capacity:

- 20 to 39 passengers

### Large Regional Turboprop

Larger turboprops for stronger short-haul regional routes.

They are useful when demand is too large for small commuter aircraft but not strong enough for regional jets.

Typical role:

- Efficient regional service
- Short-to-medium routes
- Community-to-regional airport service
- Partner feeder contracts

Approximate capacity:

- 40 to 78 passengers

### Small Regional Jet

Small jets that provide speed and passenger appeal but can be more expensive to operate than turboprops.

They should not be automatically better than turboprops. They are useful when speed, schedule quality, or partner requirements matter.

Typical role:

- Business-focused regional routes
- Partner airline feeder service
- Longer regional routes
- Routes where turboprops are too slow or less attractive

Approximate capacity:

- 37 to 50 passengers

### Large Regional Jet

Larger regional jets that form the backbone of regional affiliate and independent regional operations.

Typical role:

- Strong regional routes
- Partner contracts
- Early hub feed
- Growing independent regional service

Approximate capacity:

- 65 to 90 passengers

### Crossover Jet

Aircraft that bridge the gap between regional jets and mainline narrowbodies.

These aircraft are important for the transition from regional airline to small national carrier.

Typical role:

- Thin mainline routes
- Strong regional routes
- Early national expansion
- Routes that are too large for regional jets but too small for standard narrowbodies

Approximate capacity:

- 90 to 130 passengers

### Future Categories

These categories are future expansion targets after the early vertical trunk is stable.

- Small narrowbody
- Standard narrowbody
- Large narrowbody
- Long-range narrowbody
- Small widebody
- Medium widebody
- Large widebody

## Aircraft Families

Aircraft should be grouped into fictional families.

Families allow future mechanics such as:

- Fleet commonality
- Shared pilot training
- Shared maintenance expertise
- Parts availability
- Manufacturer loyalty
- Family-specific upgrades
- Discounted fleet deals

Example fictional families may include:

- Founder utility aircraft family
- Commuter turboprop family
- Regional turboprop family
- Regional jet family
- Crossover jet family
- Narrowbody family
- Widebody family

Names should feel plausible and serious, not parody-like.

## Aircraft Manufacturers

The game should include multiple fictional aircraft manufacturers.

Manufacturers should compete with each other by offering different strengths, weaknesses, aircraft families, financing terms, reliability profiles, and upgrade paths.

This gives aircraft decisions more texture than simply choosing the largest aircraft the airline can afford.

## Manufacturer Design Goals

Manufacturers should:

- Feel distinct without becoming parody brands
- Offer competing aircraft within similar categories
- Encourage different airline strategies
- Create tradeoffs between price, reliability, comfort, efficiency, delivery time, and financing
- Support future manufacturer loyalty, discounts, fleet deals, and launch customer opportunities

## Manufacturer Differences

Aircraft manufacturers may differ in:

- Purchase price
- Lease cost
- Fuel efficiency
- Maintenance cost
- Reliability
- Passenger comfort
- Range
- Airport performance
- Delivery time
- Used market availability
- Financing options
- Family commonality
- Upgrade paths
- Manufacturer support quality

## Competing Aircraft Options

Each aircraft category should eventually have multiple aircraft options from different manufacturers.

Example:

A commuter aircraft category may include:

- A cheaper aircraft with lower comfort and reliability
- A more expensive aircraft with better efficiency
- A rugged aircraft that performs better at smaller airports
- A newer aircraft with higher financing cost but lower operating cost

A regional jet category may include:

- A lower-cost older design
- A more efficient modern design
- A higher-comfort aircraft preferred by business passengers
- A short-field variant useful for constrained airports

The goal is not to overwhelm the player early. The game should introduce aircraft choice gradually as the airline grows.

## Manufacturer Strategy

Manufacturers can become part of the airline’s long-term strategy.

Future mechanics may include:

- Fleet commonality bonuses
- Manufacturer loyalty discounts
- Preferred customer pricing
- Launch customer contracts
- Exclusive early access to new aircraft
- Manufacturer support agreements
- Bulk order negotiations
- Delayed delivery risk
- Aircraft family upgrade paths

These systems are future-facing. Version 1.0 only needs enough manufacturer structure to support distinct aircraft options and future expansion.

## Acquisition Types

### Wet Lease

A short-term aircraft arrangement where crew and maintenance are included.

Useful for emergency capacity, short contracts, or temporary route testing.

Pros:

- Fast access
- Lower setup burden
- Useful for short-term needs

Cons:

- Expensive
- Limited customization
- Not ideal long-term

### Operating Lease

The airline pays recurring lease costs but does not own the aircraft.

Pros:

- Lower upfront cost
- Useful for growth
- Easier than buying

Cons:

- Monthly pressure
- Return restrictions
- Less long-term value

### Finance Lease / Loan Purchase

The airline pays over time and may eventually own the aircraft.

Pros:

- Builds ownership
- Can support long-term fleet planning

Cons:

- Debt risk
- Payment pressure
- Harder to reverse

### Used Purchase

The airline buys an aircraft outright from the used market.

Pros:

- Cheaper than new
- Immediate ownership
- Can be good for startups

Cons:

- Higher maintenance risk
- Lower reliability
- Older cabins
- Possible hidden costs

### New Purchase

The airline buys a new aircraft.

Pros:

- Best reliability
- Best condition
- Full configuration control
- Strong long-term value

Cons:

- Very expensive
- Delivery delay
- Not realistic for earliest stages

### Partner-Connected Aircraft

Partner-connected aircraft are aircraft involved in a partner airline agreement.

They are not always fully owned by the partner. A partner contract may allow different aircraft ownership and operation structures.

This matters because aircraft ownership affects route freedom, contract requirements, financial risk, and future separation from the partner.

## Partner Aircraft Structures

### Partner-Owned Aircraft

The partner airline owns or controls the aircraft.

The player operates the aircraft under the partner agreement.

Pros:

- Low or no acquisition cost for the player
- Enables faster growth
- May unlock larger aircraft earlier
- Useful for partner feeder routes

Cons:

- Aircraft may be lost if the partnership ends
- Aircraft may be restricted to partner routes
- Cabin layout and branding may be controlled by the partner
- Partner may impose reliability, maintenance, or usage requirements

### Player-Owned Aircraft Used for Partner Flying

The player owns or leases the aircraft and uses it to operate partner routes.

Pros:

- The player keeps the aircraft after separation
- More useful for long-term fleet growth
- Can build operational independence while earning partner revenue

Cons:

- The player carries acquisition or lease risk
- Aircraft must meet partner requirements
- Partner may require specific cabin, reliability, or aircraft class standards
- Using owned aircraft for partner flying may limit independent route capacity

### Partner-Financed Aircraft

The partner helps finance, subsidize, lease, or guarantee aircraft access.

Pros:

- Easier access to larger or newer aircraft
- Lower short-term financial pressure
- Helps the player scale quickly

Cons:

- May create dependency
- May include buyout clauses
- May restrict aircraft use
- May create penalties during separation

### Mixed Fleet Partner Contract

Some contracts may allow a mix of partner-owned, partner-financed, and player-owned aircraft.

This gives the player flexibility but makes separation more complicated.

## Partner Contract Negotiation

Partner aircraft structure should eventually be part of contract negotiation.

A partner contract may define:

- Who owns the aircraft
- Who pays aircraft costs
- Who controls cabin configuration
- Who controls branding/livery
- Which routes the aircraft may operate
- Minimum reliability requirements
- Minimum aircraft class or capacity
- Maintenance standards
- Contract duration
- Buyout terms
- Separation penalties
- Whether aircraft can be purchased or retained after the contract

Version 1.0 may use simple predefined partner contract structures, but the data model should allow future negotiation.

## Separation Logic

Partner aircraft ownership is critical for future independence and breakaway mechanics.

During separation, the game needs to know:

- Which aircraft are owned by the player
- Which aircraft are leased by the player
- Which aircraft are owned by the partner
- Which aircraft are financed or guaranteed by the partner
- Which aircraft are restricted to partner routes
- Which aircraft can be bought out
- Which aircraft must be returned
- Which routes depend on partner aircraft
- Which schedules become invalid if partner aircraft are removed

This should be modeled early enough that future breakaway mechanics do not require rewriting the aircraft system.

## Individual Aircraft Properties

Each aircraft instance should be more than just an aircraft type.

Possible properties:

- Aircraft ID
- Aircraft type
- Age
- Flight hours
- Cycles
- Condition
- Cabin condition
- Reliability
- Maintenance status
- Ownership type
- Monthly payment
- Residual value
- Assigned base
- Assigned schedule
- Cabin configuration
- Partner ownership flag
- Restrictions or contract ties

## Aircraft Strategic Lessons

Early aircraft teach survival.

Commuter aircraft teach thin-route economics.

Turboprops teach efficiency and airport fit.

Regional jets teach speed, partner requirements, and higher operating pressure.

Large regional aircraft teach scale.

Crossover jets teach the transition into mainline thinking.

Future narrowbodies teach route density, competition, and hub building.

Future widebodies teach international risk, feed, premium cabins, and long-haul strategy.
