# Game Pillars

## Story-Guided, Not Story-Locked

The game has a crafted career storyline, but the player decides when and how to progress.

The main story provides direction, unlocks, pressure, and long-term goals. It should never make the player feel trapped in a fixed sequence with no freedom.

Players can stay in a stage longer, complete side contracts, build extra routes, save money, optimize their operation, or prepare before moving forward.

## Simulation Is the World

The game world is the airline operation itself.

Airports, aircraft, routes, schedules, contracts, finances, partnerships, reputation, and competitors are the world the player explores and shapes.

Instead of walking through a 3D map, the player explores by opening routes, entering markets, building hubs, signing contracts, and expanding their airline network.

## The Airline Is the Player Character

The player's airline is the thing that grows, struggles, earns reputation, makes choices, and changes identity over time.

Progression should feel like the airline is becoming more capable, not just that the player unlocked bigger numbers.

## Progression Through Capability

The player unlocks new opportunities by proving they can operate well.

Progression should be based on things like reliability, route performance, passenger volume, contract success, cash stability, aircraft utilization, and reputation.

The game should avoid giving the player large aircraft, major hubs, or advanced systems before they understand why those things matter.

## Aircraft Teach Strategy

Aircraft are fictional and designed around gameplay roles.

Each aircraft class should teach a different lesson: tiny founder aircraft, commuter aircraft, turboprops, regional jets, crossover jets, narrowbodies, long-range aircraft, and future widebodies.

Bigger aircraft are not automatically better. Aircraft choice should depend on route demand, distance, airport limitations, operating cost, financing, passenger expectations, and schedule needs.

## Partnerships Are Ladders and Cages

Partner airlines help the player grow, especially during the regional era.

A partnership can provide routes, money, aircraft access, credibility, airport access, and passenger feed. Over time, that same partnership can limit the player's freedom, brand growth, route choices, and long-term independence.

The player should decide when to deepen a partnership, stay comfortable, prepare for independence, or break away.

## Catch-Up Runs Operations, Not Story

Operations catch-up simulates stable scheduled operations while the player is away.

Catch-up can earn money, apply costs, progress existing operations, and generate reports. It should not trigger major story decisions, accept contracts, buy aircraft, open routes, or make irreversible choices without the player.

The world keeps moving, but the story waits for the player.

## Complexity Branches From a Stable Trunk

The game is complex on purpose, but complexity should be added in layers.

The first major goal is the stable vertical trunk: founder operator, scheduled commuter, regional partner opportunity, and early affiliate growth.

Future branch features can add airport expansion, international partners, cargo, weather, alliances, subsidiaries, deeper cabin products, advanced competitors, and other systems.

A feature should either support the current trunk or be clearly marked as a future branch.
